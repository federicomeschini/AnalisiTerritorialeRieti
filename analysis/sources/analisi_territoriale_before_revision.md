# Rieti: a territorial analysis for policy makers

## The policy proposition

**Rieti should be treated as a connected but internally uneven production territory, not as a uniformly peripheral province.** Its economy contains specialised, outward-facing activities with national and international links, while its demographic and service challenge is strongly place-specific: the Sabina belt closest to Rome is growing, whereas the Rieti basin/corridor and, most sharply, the inland mountain area are shrinking and ageing.

The strategic task is therefore twofold:

1. use transport, skills, utilities and supplier-development policy to retain more value around the province's export and logistics anchors; and
2. tailor access to services, housing, mobility and energy resilience to three very different territorial contexts.

This is a presentation-ready evidence base, not a project ranking. It identifies the stories worth validating with firms, municipalities and service providers before capital is committed.

## 1. What territory are we discussing?

Rieti comprises **73 municipalities**, **149,766 residents** in 2025 and **2,750 km²**. Its population density is **54.5 inhabitants/km²**: below Viterbo (85.0), Terni (101.2) and the non-Rome Lazio municipalities contained in the supplied file (125.2). The municipality of Rieti is the principal centre, with 45,083 residents—about 30% of provincial population—but the province is fundamentally polycentric and dispersed.

The population-weighted corrected distance to Rome is **61.6 km**. That proximity is economically meaningful, but it does not produce a single territorial outcome. The available municipal indicators show three functional areas:

| Functional area | Municipalities | 2025 population | Density (inh./km²) | Population change, 2021–25 | Ageing index, 2025 | Policy reading |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Sabina / Rome-proximate belt (<55 km corrected distance) | 27 | 57,585 | 99.5 | **+1.10%** | 241.7 | The demographic gateway: potential for mobility, housing, productive sites and service capacity. |
| Rieti basin and corridor (55–85 km) | 39 | 85,840 | 57.4 | **−2.07%** | 308.8 | The provincial core, but already losing residents; it must combine jobs and everyday accessibility. |
| Inland mountain area (>85 km) | 7 | 6,341 | 9.4 | **−5.68%** | 464.4 | A small-population, large-area resilience challenge: access, care, mobility and selective economic niches matter more than scale. |

The high-mountain area holds only **4.2% of residents** but almost **one quarter of provincial land area**. This is why an average provincial policy can be ineffective: the same intervention cannot solve both a growing commuter-facing belt and a very sparse mountain territory.

## 2. Trend and peer comparison: the central tension

Between 2021 and 2025 Rieti lost **1,569 residents (−1.04%)**. Its population-weighted ageing index is **289.6** and 27.5% of residents are aged 65 or over. It is the oldest of the four Lazio provincial territories included in the municipal file. Rieti combines relatively favourable values for the file's mean-income and employment-rate measures with weaker business density and the highest unemployment rate among those four territories.

**The narrative to use:** Rieti's issue is not simply lack of proximity to opportunity. It is the conversion of proximity, productive assets and natural territory into a broader base of local firms, accessible services and reasons for younger households to remain. The contrast between the growing Sabina belt and the shrinking inland areas makes this visible.

## 3. The economic structure: more connected outward than inward

The 2022 MR-SAM records **€6.50bn** of gross output and **€2.97bn** of value added in Rieti. It depicts an economy deeply connected to external markets and supply chains:

| Flow in the MR-SAM | €m | Share of Rieti intermediate inputs / gross output |
|---|---:|---:|
| Intermediate inputs sourced within Rieti | 5.9 | 0.17% of intermediate inputs |
| Intermediate inputs from other Lazio provinces | 456.2 | 13.34% |
| Intermediate inputs from the rest of Italy | 2,401.1 | 70.24% |
| Imported intermediate inputs | 555.4 | 16.25% |
| Sales to rest-of-Italy intermediate demand | 2,801.1 | 43.07% of gross output |
| Rest-of-world exports | 729.6 | 11.22% of gross output |

The principal external intermediate-demand destinations are Rome (€331.8m), Milan (€278.3m) and Naples (€123.3m). Rieti should consequently be presented as a **node in national production networks**, not only as a local consumption economy.

There is also a warning inside this opportunity. The model estimates only €5.9m of within-province intermediate transactions, so standard “spend local” multiplier claims would be misleading: Rieti's local Type-I output multiplier is approximately 1.00 across sectors in this SAM. The development question is not to replace all external suppliers. It is to identify the few inputs, business services and transformation stages that can viably be retained locally.

## 4. Four economic stories worth building

### A. Logistics as a productive platform, not merely transit

Warehousing and transport support is Rieti's strongest productive-investment candidate in the SAM: **€608m gross output**, **€235m value added**, an output location quotient (LQ) of **3.02** relative to the Lazio mix, and **81%** of sales directed to external intermediate markets or exports. Its national Type-I output multiplier is **2.05**.

The policy story is to use this anchor to serve local manufacturing and agri-food: freight reliability, cold chain, shared warehousing, digital freight services, intermodal connections, maintenance and logistics skills. The success metric is not traffic volume alone; it is a higher share of local producers using value-adding logistics and local supplier contracts.

### B. An agri-food chain with a credible transformation step

Crop and animal production has **€332m output**, a very high **LQ of 6.05**, €191m value added and €50m in rest-of-world exports. Food, beverages and tobacco adds **€234m output**, €73m in rest-of-world exports and a national output multiplier of **2.07**.

The combined narrative is “from primary production to higher local value capture”: processing, quality/certification, storage, packaging, water efficiency, cold chain and market access. This is more credible than generic import substitution because it starts from both agricultural specialisation and a processing base.

### C. A compact advanced-manufacturing export base

Pharmaceuticals, machinery and electronics together account for **€519m output** and **€306m rest-of-world exports**. Each is more concentrated in Rieti's output mix than in Lazio: LQ **3.56** for pharmaceuticals, **5.17** for machinery and **5.76** for electronics.

This supports a selective industrial offer—not a broad industrial-park proposition. Priorities are dependable energy and utilities, technical training, engineering and testing links, supplier qualification, logistics, and export/innovation support. A useful test for each action is whether it helps existing firms invest, hire skilled people or procure a higher-value local service.

### D. Construction and territorial services: a resilience economy

Construction is the largest output activity (**€766m**) and has a national output multiplier of **2.35**. Health is a major place-based anchor (**€443m output**, €301m of modelled government final demand); care and education add further territorial-service capacity. These should not be sold as export sectors. They are the infrastructure of a dispersed and older province.

The investable story is a targeted resilience programme: seismic safety, energy retrofit, accessible housing, telecare and local access points. It couples construction capability to the service needs revealed by the demographic geography.

### Which sectors combine market potential and multiplier effects?

The SAM cannot forecast growth: it is a 2022 accounting model, not a sector time series. It can screen for sectors in which an expansion has a plausible market base and a material ripple through Italian supply chains. A multiplier-enabled opportunity score gives 40% weight to national output and value-added multipliers, and the remainder to scale, specialisation and external-market reach.

| Growth-and-propagation candidate | Opportunity score | National output multiplier | National value added per €1 demand | Presentation message |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Warehousing & transport support | 85.2 | 2.05 | €0.83 | The strongest combined platform opportunity. |
| Crop & animal production | 81.1 | 1.85 | €0.90 | Build the processing and logistics layer around a major specialisation. |
| Machinery & equipment | 79.6 | 2.00 | €0.65 | A focused tradable-manufacturing and skills proposition. |
| Electronics & optical products | 75.3 | 1.70 | €0.63 | A specialised niche requiring targeted technical capability. |
| Pharmaceuticals | 73.1 | 1.56 | €0.59 | Strong export base; focus on skilled jobs, utilities and supplier services. |
| Food, beverages & tobacco | 66.1 | 2.07 | €0.63 | The priority agri-food transformation link. |

Construction (2.35 output multiplier) and electricity/gas (2.55, the highest) have very strong propagation effects, but are better positioned as resilience and enabling-infrastructure investments than as stand-alone export-growth sectors. Every multiplier above is a **national** Type-I effect; Rieti retains very little indirect activity in the SAM. A project should therefore pass both tests: a strong market-plus-multiplier case and a credible plan to retain a specific supplier, processing or business-service function locally.

## 5. Energy resilience: a targeted overlay, not the headline

The energy index covers the 73 Rieti municipalities and 30,214 municipal employees in 2022. Employee-weighted integrated energy risk is at the **47th percentile** in the supplied index, compared with the employee-weighted **40th percentile** for the Lazio municipalities in the file. The index is driven mainly by its non-electricity component (2.28 points of a 2.58 employee-weighted raw score).

Risk is not evenly distributed. **7,900 employees (26.1%)** work in municipalities classified high or very high risk. The meaningful employment concentrations are Fara in Sabina (4,852 employees; 96th percentile), Cittaducale (2,465; 95th) and Montopoli di Sabina (494; 95th). By contrast, the municipality of Rieti has 12,254 employees and a low-risk classification (31st percentile).

This suggests a practical overlay on industrial and municipal investment: carry out energy-cost and resilience audits for firms/sites in the high-risk employment municipalities; prioritise efficiency, self-consumption where feasible, storage/flexibility and energy-management capability. The index should be used to target diagnosis and project development—not to infer firm-level energy bills or to rank individual municipalities without validation.

## 6. SWOT: a decision-oriented synthesis

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Specialised external-facing base: logistics, agriculture, food, pharmaceuticals, machinery and electronics. | Very limited within-province intermediate circulation in the SAM; indirect benefits leak largely outside the province. |
| Material external markets: €730m rest-of-world exports and strong connections with Rome, Milan and Naples. | Low density, 73 municipalities and pronounced spatial fragmentation raise service-delivery and coordination costs. |
| Rome-proximate Sabina municipalities are growing, offering a demographic and business-development foothold. | Population decline overall, very high ageing, and sharp decline in the inland mountain group. |
| Construction and health/care capacity can support a resilience and ageing-in-place strategy. | Business density is lower than the comparison provinces; unemployment is the highest of the Lazio provincial territories contained in the file. |

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| Build supplier, engineering, processing, cold-chain and digital-logistics capabilities around existing anchors. | Treating the province as a uniform “inner area” could divert resources from the distinct growth needs of Sabina and the service-access needs of mountain municipalities. |
| Link advanced manufacturing and logistics investment to skills, reliable utilities and energy resilience. | Energy-cost exposure is concentrated in several important employment municipalities, potentially weakening investment and competitiveness. |
| Deploy retrofit, seismic safety, accessible housing and telecare as a combined construction–service programme. | A widening demographic gap between the Rome-facing belt and inland territory could weaken fiscal capacity and everyday service access. |
| Use Rome proximity as a market and talent connection while improving the conditions for local value retention. | Chasing generic “local multipliers” could subsidise uncompetitive substitution rather than viable supplier development. |

## 7. A policy agenda that follows the evidence

1. **Make a three-zone territorial pact.** Use a shared provincial strategy, but with different portfolios: growth management and productive sites in Sabina; jobs, services and urban/corridor accessibility around Rieti; minimum-access, care, mobility and heritage/niche-economy offers in the mountain area.
2. **Launch three value-retention pilots.** Test an agri-food processing/cold-chain platform, a logistics-producer services programme and an advanced-manufacturing supplier/skills programme. Start with named demand, anchor firms and a small number of missing capabilities.
3. **Connect industrial and energy policy.** Begin with energy-risk audits and investable projects in Fara in Sabina, Cittaducale and Montopoli di Sabina, then connect support to productivity, resilience and local skills outcomes.
4. **Use construction as an outcome vehicle.** Prioritise projects that simultaneously improve seismic/energy performance and make older residents' access to housing, health and care more sustainable.
5. **Measure outcomes, not only spending.** Track population by functional zone; travel time to essential services; business births and firms per resident; local supplier spend of anchor firms; export and processed-agri-food value; energy cost/resilience outcomes; and skilled jobs/apprenticeships.

## 8. How to present this in 10 slides

1. The headline: connected production territory, uneven demographic geography.
2. The map: three functional areas, with population change and ageing.
3. The peer benchmark: density, demographic trend, ageing, business density and labour market.
4. The SAM: Rieti's outward connections and the local-retention challenge.
5. Logistics as platform economy.
6. Agri-food from primary production to transformation.
7. Advanced manufacturing: a focused export-and-skills proposition.
8. Construction, health and care as the resilience economy.
9. Energy risk overlay: target the high-risk employment municipalities.
10. SWOT, five priorities and a 12-month validation agenda.

## Evidence, definitions and limits

- Municipal indicators are from `New_Query_2026_09_30_10_19_18.csv`: demographic figures are 2021/2025, income and labour-market variables are 2023, and municipal finance/land-use variables have their supplied dates. Province and zone figures in this note are sums or population-weighted averages of municipal values.
- The peer table is deliberately limited to comparable provincial territories available in the supplied file. That file contains Abruzzo, Lazio (without the Rome metropolitan province), Molise and Umbria; it is **not a national benchmark**.
- Economic structure comes from the 2022 interregional SAM and its derived sector indicators in `SAM/output/`. It is a modelled accounting framework, not a census of firm transactions. National final-demand accounts are not physically located within Rieti and should not be presented as local spending.
- Energy figures are from `municipality_integrated_energy_risk_latest_inputs.csv` for 2022. They are municipal composite risk indicators, not direct observations of firm bills or renewable potential.
- Before publication or project selection, validate anchor sectors and constraints with firm/employment registers, Chamber of Commerce data, freight and commuting evidence, service travel times, municipal plans, grid/utility data and interviews with leading employers.
